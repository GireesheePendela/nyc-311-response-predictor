How long will your 311 complaint take?

I looked at every 311 complaint New Yorkers filed and the city closed in the first two months of 2026 to see what actually decides how long you wait.

It depends mostly on what you report

Half of all complaints are closed within about 12 hours, but that average hides two very different worlds. Noise and parking complaints go to the NYPD and are usually closed within an hour or two. Housing problems go to the Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) and take days: a typical heat complaint takes about two days, and an unsanitary-conditions complaint more than a week.

The Bronx isn't slow at fixing heat

Across all complaints, the Bronx and Manhattan look slowest, taking over 20 hours at the median compared with about 4 in Staten Island and Queens. But that is mostly about what each borough reports. Half of Bronx complaints are housing complaints, compared with about 1 in 10 on Staten Island. Compare like with like, heat complaints only, and the Bronx is actually the fastest borough while Brooklyn is the slowest.

Using the app won't speed up a heat complaint

Complaints filed through the 311 mobile app close in under 3 hours at the median, and phone complaints take about 35. It's tempting to conclude the app is faster. But two-thirds of app complaints are police matters like noise and parking, which are quick no matter how you file. For heat complaints, the channel barely matters: all three land within a few hours of each other, around two days.

Estimate your own complaint

I trained a model on these complaints to estimate how long a new one will take, based on the problem type, borough, how you file it, and when. It explains about 78% of the variation in (log) resolution time. On complaints it never saw, its average miss is about 35 hours, so use it to compare situations, not as a promise.

What "closed" does and doesn't mean

  • Closed isn't always fixed. An agency closes a request when it has acted on it, which can mean an inspection was scheduled, a violation was issued, or no problem was found when someone arrived.
  • It's a winter snapshot. The data covers January 1 to March 2, 2026, peak heating season, which is why heat complaints dominate. Summer would look different.
  • Medians, not guarantees. Every number here is a median, which means half of complaints took longer.
  • Only closed complaints count. About 96,000 requests in the export were still open or in progress and are not included.

How I built it

  1. Exported 692,052 service requests created January 1 – March 2, 2026 from the 311 Service Requests dataset on NYC Open Data.
  2. Loaded them into PostgreSQL and kept 589,802 closed complaints with valid timestamps (dropping negative durations and anything over a year).
  3. Explored response times by complaint type, agency, borough, channel, and time of day with SQL.
  4. Built features from the filing time, agency, borough, and channel, and encoded complaint type using training data only to avoid leaking the answer into the model.
  5. Compared linear regression, random forest, and XGBoost on a validation set; the random forest won and was scored once on held-out data.
  6. Shipped the model and dashboard as a free web app on Hugging Face Spaces.

All code, including the script that computes every number on this page (step9_gallery_data.py), is on GitHub with step-by-step instructions to reproduce it.
